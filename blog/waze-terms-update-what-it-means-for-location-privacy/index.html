Waze Terms Update: What It Means for Location Privacy

Waze's updated terms and privacy policy are a good reminder that navigation apps collect sensitive location data, operate across borders, and deserve a practical privacy review.

Published on July 13, 2026 at 10:44am by Kyle Reddoch • 8 min read
Permalink

Waze is one of those apps that we use without thinking twice about the implications.

You open it because traffic is bad, the route looks smarter than whatever your car suggests, and you would rather know about the crash before you are sitting behind it. That kind of usefulness lowers our guard. The app helps right now. The privacy tradeoff sits quietly in the background.

That is why Waze’s recent terms update deserves a sober look. Not a panic cycle, and not a hand-wave either. A navigation app is not just another free utility. It handles location history, route patterns, device signals, advertising context, and account data. When the terms and privacy policy change, the right response is to read them like they apply to something sensitive, because they do.

Waze’s Terms of Use and Privacy Policy both show a July 8, 2026 update. The language getting the most attention is the legal home of the service. Waze says the service is provided by Waze Mobile Ltd., a company incorporated and operating under the laws of Israel, and the terms say disputes are governed by Israeli law and handled in the competent court in the Tel Aviv district.

That does not mean an Israeli government agency is sitting inside your phone watching your commute. That is not what the policy says, and turning every jurisdiction clause into a surveillance headline does not help anyone make better security decisions. The more accurate concern is narrower and more useful: the legal structure behind a location-heavy app is international, and users should understand what data exists, who controls it, where it may be processed, and how lawful requests are handled.

Location data is not ordinary app data

The sensitive part of Waze is not only that it knows where you are right now. It is that navigation data becomes a pattern.

Waze’s privacy policy says the app may collect detailed location, travel, and route information, including GPS and sensor data, timestamps, IP address, and other device-adjacent signals. It also says this can be saved to a route history of journeys made while using the app. Depending on device and account settings, Waze says it may collect location and travel information even when you are not actively using the app, for things like planned drive reminders, traffic notifications, and parking assistance.

That is enough context to tell a story about a person without ever reading a message or opening a photo library. Over time, route data can suggest:

  • Where you live.
  • Where you work.
  • When you usually leave.
  • Which school, clinic, church, or gym you visit.
  • Which family members or clients you may be near.
  • When your home is probably empty.
  • Which routes are routine and which trips are unusual.

For most people, none of that is dramatic on a single drive. The issue is aggregation. Location history is one of the easiest data sets to underestimate because each point feels mundane until the points describe a routine.

The Israel part matters, but it needs careful framing

Jurisdiction clauses are not rare. Plenty of apps name a governing law and venue, and most users accept those terms without reading past the first screen. The reason this one feels different is the class of data involved.

Waze is not a calculator app. It is a navigation app owned by Google but legally provided by Waze Mobile Ltd. in Israel. Its updated terms identify the service provider in Tel Aviv, and its privacy policy says the data controller responsible for user information is Waze Mobile Ltd. in Israel.

That means users should separate a few questions instead of collapsing everything into one vague fear:

  • Who provides the service?
  • Where can the data be processed or stored?
  • Which company is the controller for the data?
  • Which legal processes could apply if a government or court seeks data?

Waze says data may be processed on servers around the world and that data protection laws vary by country. It also says Waze may share information when required by law, when it reasonably believes disclosure is required, or when it needs to protect Waze, users, or the public. The policy also notes that Waze is Google-owned and may share personal information with Google affiliates, including de-identified account settings, location and route-related data, and metadata for mapping and navigation improvements.

That is not the same as saying “Israel can see everything.” It is also not nothing.

Google’s own policy on government requests for user information says government agencies around the world ask Google for user information, that Google reviews requests, tries to narrow overbroad demands, and sometimes objects. Google also says it does not provide backdoor direct access and does not sell personal information to governments. Those statements matter, but they describe a process, not a world where legal demands never happen.

From a security perspective, that is the practical takeaway. Jurisdiction matters. Legal process matters. Data minimization matters. If an app collects fewer sensitive signals, there is less to request, breach, misuse, subpoena, aggregate, or misunderstand later.

Invisible mode is not the same as private mode

One detail in Waze’s privacy policy is worth underlining because it is exactly the kind of setting people can misread. Waze says Invisible Mode can stop other users from seeing your location on the map, but it also says your location and route information will continue to be collected by Waze for the purposes listed in the policy.

That is a privacy control, but it is not a private mode. It may reduce social visibility inside the app. It does not make Waze stop collecting route data. If your concern is other drivers seeing you, Invisible Mode may help. If your concern is collection by the service provider, it is not enough by itself.

Ads are part of the tradeoff

Waze is free because advertising pays for it. The terms say Waze uses data to show ads according to user settings, and the privacy policy says ads can be based on current activity such as approximate location, the general path of a route, destination type, and other drive-session context.

The policy also says personalized ads may be based on information provided to Waze, past activity and search history, and, depending on settings, Google Account profile and activity.

That does not make Waze uniquely bad. It makes Waze a modern free app with a high-value data set. The professional risk view is simple enough: the more personal the data, the more carefully a user should treat settings, account access, and retention.

What I would do if you keep using Waze

If Waze is useful enough that you want to keep it, I would not treat that as an unreasonable choice. I would treat it like a sensitive app rather than a casual one.

  • Review Waze’s in-app Privacy Settings.
  • Turn off ads personalization if you do not want route and activity history used for personalized ads.
  • Avoid saving Home and Work if you do not need those shortcuts.
  • Use your phone’s location permission controls and consider “While Using” instead of broader background access.
  • Review connected third-party services like music apps.
  • Be careful with reports, profile details, and any content that may be visible to other users.
  • Use Invisible Mode only for user-to-user visibility, not as a full privacy control.
  • Download your data archive occasionally if you want to understand what is tied to the account.

For higher-risk users, the answer may be different. Journalists, activists, public officials, people dealing with stalking or domestic abuse risk, sensitive business travelers, or anyone with a strong threat model around location data should think harder about whether any crowd-powered navigation app belongs on their phone.

That is not because Waze is uniquely suspicious. It is because precise movement data is sensitive by design, and the risk level is not the same for every person.

How to delete your Waze account

If you decide the app is not worth the privacy tradeoff, delete the account before deleting the app.

Waze says you can delete your account through the “Delete Account” function in the in-app Privacy Settings or Account & Login Settings. The privacy policy says you can also use the Waze website, and that once the request is received, Waze begins a process designed to delete the data from its storage systems. Waze says that process generally takes around two months, and that backup systems, maintenance, outages, bugs, or legal and business retention requirements can affect deletion timing.

Before deleting, consider downloading your data:

  1. Go to the Waze website.
  2. Sign in.
  3. Open Settings.
  4. Choose the option to download your data archive.

Then delete the account:

  1. Open the Waze app.
  2. Go to Settings.
  3. Open Privacy Settings or Account & Login.
  4. Choose Delete Account.
  5. Follow the confirmation prompts.

After that, remove the app from your phone.

On iPhone, long-press the Waze app icon, choose Remove App, then Delete App.

On Android, long-press the Waze app icon and choose Uninstall, or open Settings, go to Apps, select Waze, and choose Uninstall.

If you are not deleting the account but want to reduce exposure, leave the app installed only if you also review location permissions, notification settings, connected services, and ad personalization.

Businesses should treat this like a data governance issue

For businesses, MSPs, and small teams, this should be less about whether one employee likes Waze and more about whether route history creates business risk.

Do employees use personal navigation apps for client visits, medical facilities, legal offices, executive travel, high-value deliveries, or sensitive work sites? Are they saving customer locations as favorites? Are screenshots of routes being shared in chats or tickets? Are location permissions always-on because nobody reviewed them?

Those are normal operational questions. A company does not need a Waze panic policy. It may need a location data policy.

That policy can be simple:

  • Use approved navigation tools for business travel where needed.
  • Avoid saving sensitive client locations in personal apps.
  • Do not share route screenshots that expose customer names, addresses, or schedules.
  • Review app permissions on managed devices.
  • Consider whether high-risk roles need stricter location app guidance.

Most privacy problems are not caused by one scary clause. They are caused by useful tools becoming quiet databases.

The bottom line

The Waze update is a reminder to read the boring parts. The useful app on your phone is also a location data system, an advertising-supported service, a Google-owned product, and a contract with an Israeli company governed by Israeli law.

That does not make it malicious. It does make it worth understanding.

If Waze still earns its place on your phone, tighten the settings and be intentional about what you share. If it does not, delete the account first, then delete the app.

The privacy question is not whether Waze is good or bad. The better question is whether the convenience is worth the location history you are creating.
