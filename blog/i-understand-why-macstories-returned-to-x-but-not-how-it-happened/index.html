I Understand Why MacStories Returned to X, but Not How It Happened

I understand why MacStories returned to X. My concern is how the decision was handled and communicated to its team, members, and wider community.

Published on August 22, 2026 at 11:38am by Kyle Reddoch • 9 min read
Permalink

This is a little outside the kind of thing I normally write about here, but I have been following the discussion around MacStories returning to Twitter/X over the last couple of days, and I kept finding myself thinking about it.

Federico Viticci is someone I have followed for years through MacStories. Obviously there is the Apple connection, along with his work around automation, Shortcuts, apps, and more recently AI and coding, but I also have a lot of respect for what he and the MacStories team have managed to build independently on the web. MacStories has been around for well over a decade, has its own membership program, podcasts, newsletters, and a community that exists because the team built something people wanted to keep coming back to.

I still enjoy reading MacStories, and nothing about this situation changes the respect I have for the work Federico and the rest of the team do. As someone putting more work into my own little corner of the web, I probably appreciate what they have built more than I used to.

There is also a personal reason this particular situation caught my attention. I left Twitter around the time Elon Musk took over, which was around the same period MacStories stepped away from the platform and moved more of its social presence toward Mastodon. At the time, that made a lot of sense to me because I had reached many of the same conclusions myself.

I have since reactivated my X account, although I have not really started using it again. It is there, but for now that is about the extent of it. That makes it especially important to be clear about what bothers me here: I am not arguing that MacStories has no right to return to X, or that using the platform makes Federico a hypocrite. My concern is the way the return happened and how it was communicated. Reactivating a personal account is one thing. Turning that into part of the public strategy for a publication with a team, paying members, and a community around it is another.

So when Federico suddenly appeared back on X, my reaction was basically, well, that’s interesting. Judging by the discussion that followed, I was not the only person wondering what had changed.

Why MacStories Went Back

Federico Viticci and John Voorhees eventually published an explanation of the decision, and I think it is worth reading before forming an opinion based entirely on the reactions around it. The core of their explanation is understandable: MacStories is not just Federico’s blog or a side project anymore. It is a business, and it is how the people behind it make their living.

Federico’s work has also broadened over the last few years. He is still very much an Apple writer, but he is spending more time around AI, coding, automation, developer tools, and building software himself. Many of the developers, potential readers, sponsors, customers, and communities connected to that work are still active on X, whether those of us who left the platform like that fact or not.

If you run an independent publication and realize that people who might read, support, or work with you are somewhere you have intentionally chosen not to be, eventually you have to decide whether staying away is still worth what it may be costing the business. I understand why MacStories reached the conclusion it did. My own account reactivation does not mean I have decided to participate actively on X again, but it does mean I cannot pretend the calculation is simple or that I would never revisit it myself.

That is why I do not see the decision to return as some automatic betrayal of everything MacStories previously said about the open web. A website, podcast, newsletter, or membership program can remain the real home while a social network functions as another place to distribute and discuss the work.

My Problem Is How It Happened

My issue is the sequence of events and the communication around them. Federico had already returned to X before the explanation appeared, and that explanation was published as a standalone Craft document rather than an article on MacStories or a post for Club MacStories members. The explanation also made clear that the return had not been fully discussed across the whole team beforehand.

Federico is free to use any social network he wants personally, and he certainly does not need my permission. Once the move is explained as a business decision for MacStories, though, it is fair to ask whether the people working in that business were part of the conversation and why the publication’s members and wider community had to piece together what happened before hearing directly from MacStories.

Those were the questions I raised in reply to Matthew Cassinelli. I asked why the return happened before the team was aligned, why the explanation lived on Craft instead of MacStories or inside the Club, and why the community learned about it after the fact.

Matthew’s first reply and second reply interpreted those questions as wanting Federico to seek my approval or damage his own business to satisfy critics. That is not what I was saying. I do not think Federico should put MacStories at risk to satisfy every person on Mastodon, and I am not claiming a vote in how the company operates. A member-supported independent publication can make its own business decision and still handle the communication around that decision poorly.

The history matters here because MacStories did not leave Twitter after simply losing interest in the platform. Its departure came as Twitter was changing under Musk, and the move toward Mastodon fit naturally with the ideas MacStories had promoted around independent publishing, open platforms, and owning more of your presence online. MacStories later stepped away from Threads and Instagram following Meta’s changes around moderation.

When choices like those are explained publicly through values, people are naturally going to notice when the direction changes. That does not make Federico a hypocrite. Circumstances, businesses, audiences, and the Internet itself all change. It does mean the explanation deserves the same care and visibility as the original position. Asking for that is not the same as asking for control.

There are also legitimate concerns about what a return to X contributes to the platform. X benefits when writers, developers, journalists, and creators post there because those people make the platform worth opening. I do not think we should pretend otherwise, but there is still a large gap between acknowledging that benefit and arguing that anyone who uses X endorses Elon Musk or everything the platform has become.

Why I Am Still Supporting MacStories

I want to be equally clear about what this disagreement does not change. I am still going to read MacStories, follow what Federico and the team create, and support their work. I enjoy their articles and the broader collection of podcasts, newsletters, automation coverage, app coverage, and independent projects they have built around the site. One situation I think was handled badly does not erase years of work I value.

Manton Reece wrote about the reaction, including people talking about unfollowing Federico or canceling Club MacStories memberships. Everyone is free to decide what they want to support financially, and if a publication makes a decision that crosses a personal line, nobody is obligated to keep paying.

That is not where I land. If the goal is to support independent publishing and move more of the web away from giant centralized platforms, withdrawing support from an independent publication over one distribution decision may hurt the exact kind of publishing we say we want to survive. X will not notice that someone stopped reading or supporting MacStories, but MacStories will.

We constantly say that writers and creators should own their websites, build audiences outside social platforms, use RSS, publish newsletters, and create sustainable businesses that do not depend entirely on advertising or algorithms. MacStories has actually done those things. Continuing to support that work does not require me to agree with every decision Federico makes, just as criticizing this decision does not require me to reject everything MacStories has built.

In fact, I think support and criticism can coexist pretty comfortably here. I care about how MacStories handled the situation because I respect the publication and the community around it. If I did not value the work, there would not be much reason for me to spend this much time thinking about it.

Mastodon and the Open Web Have Work to Do Too

This situation also raises a question that the Fediverse cannot completely avoid: why did MacStories feel the need to go back?

It is easy to answer that by saying X is bigger, but there is more to it. I like Mastodon and still use it. I would much rather participate in a network built around open protocols and independent servers than one controlled entirely by a single company. Mastodon is not perfect, though, and pretending it is does not help.

Discovery remains difficult, communities can be hard to find, and onboarding is not particularly friendly for people who do not already understand what an instance is or why they must choose one before creating an account. Parts of the community can also become hostile toward people who use the “wrong” technology, talk positively about AI, use certain social platforms, or disagree with whatever consensus has formed in that corner of the Fediverse.

None of that makes Mastodon bad. It means the alternatives to centralized social networks still have work to do. If Federico looks around and realizes that many of the developers and communities he wants to interact with remain on X, yelling at him for noticing the problem will not solve it. The better response is to make the open alternatives useful and welcoming enough that independent publishers do not feel they are missing something by staying away from centralized platforms.

Where I Land on It

I have not really gone back to X myself. I reactivated my account, but I have not started posting there again and still have not decided what role, if any, I want it to have in what I do online. The reasons I left have not disappeared, and reactivating the account did not change how I feel about the direction the platform took. At the same time, I would be lying if I said I did not understand the pressures Federico described. People and conversations still exist there that are not happening in quite the same way elsewhere.

For my own work, I remain much more interested in things I have some ownership over. My website, RSS feeds, projects, and newsletter give me ways to publish and reach people without depending entirely on somebody else’s timeline. Social networks can disappear or change direction while the work on my own site remains.

The same is true for MacStories. It has not moved its articles to X or made the platform its new home. Its writing, memberships, podcasts, newsletters, and archives still exist in places MacStories controls far more than it could ever control a social-media account. X is another place where the team has chosen to distribute and talk about that work, and I understand the business reasoning behind using it.

What I disagree with is how the return was carried out. MacStories could have discussed the decision internally first, explained it on its own site or directly to its members, and let the community hear about it from the publication instead of discovering the return and receiving the explanation afterward. None of that would have required Federico to ask the Fediverse for permission or give critics a veto over the business.

None of this changes my plans to keep reading MacStories, following what Federico and the team make, and hoping the publication continues to succeed. I can believe Federico handled this situation poorly without turning that criticism into a verdict on everything he and the team have built. That is where I land: I understand why MacStories returned to X, I disagree with how it happened, and I still support the work.
