Sora is both interesting and scary. Interesting because it lets anyone direct short, realistic videos with synced audio on a phone. Scary because it turns identity itself into a shareable asset other people can cast. Those two truths sit together in one app.

OpenAI says Sora 2 improves realism, physics, and instruction following, and it ships inside a social app that is rolling out now in the United States and Canada. Downloads include a visible moving watermark and C2PA provenance metadata to signal that a clip was generated by Sora. That is a stronger baseline than most consumer tools so far ([OpenAI: “Sora 2 is here”](https://openai.com/index/sora-2/); [Sora landing](https://openai.com/sora/); [Launching Sora responsibly](https://openai.com/index/launching-sora-responsibly/); [Getting started guide](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12456897-getting-started-with-the-sora-app)).

## What Sora does in practice

- **Short, directed clips with audio.** You write a prompt with camera hints and timing. Sora renders a 10–15 second video with synchronized sound and natural motion. OpenAI’s system card and launch post emphasize better physics and steerability compared with earlier models ([System card](https://openai.com/index/sora-2-system-card/); [Sora 2 announcement](https://openai.com/index/sora-2/)).
- **Cameos of real people.** This is the headline feature. You record a selfie capture to create a reusable, permissioned “cameo” of yourself that can be cast by others you allow ([Cameo help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12435986-generating-content-with-cameos)).
- **Provenance signals.** Sora adds a visible moving watermark and embeds C2PA credentials in the file to help downstream verification. C2PA is the open standard for content authenticity metadata and is being adopted across cameras, newsrooms, and AI tools ([Launching responsibly](https://openai.com/index/launching-sora-responsibly/); [Getting started](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12456897-getting-started-with-the-sora-app); [C2PA explainer](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8912793-c2pa-in-chatgpt-images)).

## Cameos: the best feature and the riskiest concept

You can control who is allowed to feature your cameo:

- **Only me**  
- **People I approve**  
- **Mutuals**  
- **Everyone**  

You can also set Cameo rules, review drafts that include you, revoke access going forward, and remove videos that use your cameo. Teen accounts are limited to stricter settings by default ([Cameo help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12435986-generating-content-with-cameos); [Getting started](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12456897-getting-started-with-the-sora-app)).

The consent model is better than the free-for-all deepfake scene, but there is still social risk. You do not pre-approve each individual video before someone generates it. You can delete or report afterward, yet someone may have seen or screen-recorded it already. Reporting from reviewers highlights exactly this dynamic: it feels fun until a friend uses your face in a way you did not expect, then you realize the guardrails are reactive rather than preventive ([Washington Post analysis](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/10/07/sora-cameo-deepfake-consent/)).

## Guardrails, public figures, and the moving policy line

OpenAI claims it blocks depictions of public figures unless those people opt in through cameo. The company has also said it is working with rightsholders for more granular control over copyrighted characters ([Launching responsibly](https://openai.com/index/launching-sora-responsibly/)). Reality is evolving in public. After a wave of controversial videos of deceased figures, OpenAI paused depictions of Martin Luther King Jr. and said estates of other figures can request an opt-out. This is a live example of how safety policy is adjusting to real misuse pressure in days, not months ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/sora-martin-luther-king-jr-openai-pause-ai-video-2025-10); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/siladityaray/2025/10/17/openai-blocks-sora-deepfakes-of-martin-luther-king-after-disrespectful-depictions/); [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/news/801539/open-ai-sora-mlk)).

Other reporting has examined Sora’s ability to mimic popular formats and styles, raising questions about training data, rightsholder control, and accountability once videos begin to spread across platforms ([Washington Post training-data investigation](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/interactive/2025/openai-training-data-sora/)).

## The infosec angle: what changes when Sora goes mainstream

Think of Sora as a deepfake pipeline that regular people will actually use. That shift matters more than any single model metric. Here is how the threat model changes.

### 1) Identity and reputation attacks get easier to stage
Highly believable short clips of a co-worker, candidate, or journalist can be made quickly with their own consented cameo or with a convincing look-alike if you try to skirt rules. Family members of deceased celebrities are already reporting harm and harassment from viral Sora videos, which is a canary for reputational attacks in other contexts ([Washington Post report on dead-celebrity videos](https://img.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/10/11/openai-sora-dead-celebrities-ai/)).

**Practical response:** write a short synthetic-media policy. Define how your team labels AI video, what to do when a malicious clip appears, and who confirms authenticity before any public comment.

### 2) Watermarks help but do not absolve
Visible marks can be cropped or obscured in re-uploads. C2PA helps when you have the original file, but many social platforms still strip or mangle metadata during transcode. Treat provenance as a necessary verification step, not a guarantee that the public will see the signal in feeds ([Launching responsibly](https://openai.com/index/launching-sora-responsibly/); [C2PA explainer](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8912793-c2pa-in-chatgpt-images)).

**Practical response:** keep master copies with credentials intact. Use C2PA-aware tools to verify when possible. Publish originals from your own domain or platform that preserves credentials, then reference that canonical copy when rumors start.

### 3) Social engineering content will upgrade fast
Sora can output short, “native” vertical clips with audio that feel like platform content. Expect more convincing vishing pretexts, fake executive messages, and crisis videos.

**Practical response:** train on real vs. Sora examples. Teach people to trust source and provenance over vibes. Incorporate Sora-style prompts into red-team scenarios.

### 4) Consent friction is real, even with options
Cameo privacy settings let you choose Only me, People I approve, Mutuals, or Everyone. They are good defaults, but they rely on users to curate allow-lists and revoke access quickly when something goes sideways. Reviewers have already shown the gap between “I can remove it later” and “damage is done” ([Cameo help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12435986-generating-content-with-cameos); [Washington Post analysis](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/10/07/sora-cameo-deepfake-consent/)).

**Practical response:** default to Only me while you learn the app. Move to People I approve only for specific collaborators. Refresh the allow-list regularly. Add simple cameo rules in plain language so expectations are baked into the asset.

### 5) Policy will keep shifting
The MLK pause shows how quickly OpenAI will adjust settings when harm becomes obvious, and it shows how public figures and estates will press for stronger veto power over likeness. Expect more toggles and special-case enforcement as rightsholders negotiate what “consent” means at platform scale ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/sora-martin-luther-king-jr-openai-pause-ai-video-2025-10); [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/news/801539/open-ai-sora-mlk)).

**Practical response:** do not assume today’s moderation results will match tomorrow’s. When you document risk, acknowledge that model behavior and policy may change mid-campaign.

## What it is like to actually use Sora

From a creator’s chair, Sora works best when you give it clear, short direction and two or three beats per clip. Lip sync and micro-expressions are surprisingly personal with cameo recordings. Crowded scenes and very fast camera moves still stumble, which matches OpenAI’s own notes on known limitations ([Sora 2 announcement](https://openai.com/index/sora-2/); [System card](https://openai.com/index/sora-2-system-card/)).

## Recommendations for teams, schools, and civil organizations

1. **Publish an authenticity policy**  
   Promise to disclose when a video is AI-generated, and explain your verification steps when debunking a fake.

2. **Use provenance end to end**  
   Keep originals with C2PA. Host a canonical copy where credentials survive. When posting elsewhere, point back to that source ([C2PA explainer](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8912793-c2pa-in-chatgpt-images)).

3. **Run a synthetic-media drill**  
   Scenario: a fake video of your spokesperson starts circulating. Who verifies, who responds, and where will you post the canonical correction?

4. **Cameo hygiene**  
   Default to **Only me**. If you open to **People I approve**, keep the list tight and review it monthly. Add Cameo rules that forbid sensitive topics or political themes ([Cameo help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12435986-generating-content-with-cameos)).

5. **Track policy changes**  
   Subscribe to official updates and credible tech press. The MLK pause is not the last adjustment you will see ([Launching responsibly](https://openai.com/index/launching-sora-responsibly/); [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/sora-martin-luther-king-jr-openai-pause-ai-video-2025-10)).

## Where this leaves us

Sora is a creative rocket and a reputational risk amplifier. The watermark and C2PA story is a meaningful start, not a shield. The cameo system gives consent levers we have not had before in consumer video tools, yet consent still collides with virality. If you create with Sora, build provenance into your workflow and publish with context. If you protect people or brands, assume Sora-quality fakes are already part of your threat model.
