A child’s first email address might not sound like a cybersecurity topic, but I think it absolutely is.

In security, we talk all the time about identity, access, exposure, and long-term risk. An email address sits right in the middle of all four. It becomes a login, a recovery method, a communication channel, and eventually part of someone’s permanent digital footprint. That is why Proton’s new [Born Private campaign](https://go.getproton.me/SH2V9) caught my attention.

This is not really about getting kids online earlier. In my view, it is about giving them a cleaner start when they do get there.

## The real issue is digital identity, not just email

Most people think about a child’s first email address as a convenience issue. Can they get a good name? Will it still be available later? That part matters, sure, but I think the bigger point is what kind of ecosystem that address is tied to from day one.

Too many online services are built around surveillance by default. Data collection is normal. Profiling is normal. Behavioral tracking is normal. That should bother us even more when the person entering that ecosystem is a kid.

That is part of why this campaign works. Proton is framing the first inbox as something worth protecting before it ever becomes active. I think that is the right lens.

## What Proton is actually offering

With Born Private, Proton lets a parent or guardian reserve a child’s email address and hold it for up to 15 years. The reserved account stays inactive until it is intentionally activated later. Proton says the inbox stays sealed while reserved, with no data, no activity logs, and no profiling. When the child is ready, the parent can use a secure voucher to activate it. Proton also says reservations start with a small donation beginning at $1, partly to reduce abuse and fraud and to support the Proton Foundation.

That matters because it shifts the model. Instead of creating a live account immediately and hoping privacy settings are good enough, the address can be preserved first and activated later when the family decides the timing is right.

## Why this matters from a cybersecurity perspective

From a cybersecurity standpoint, this campaign lands for three reasons.

First, email is identity infrastructure. It is not just a mailbox. It becomes the backbone for account creation, password resets, school communications, app signups, and family coordination. Starting that identity with a privacy-focused provider is a meaningful decision.

Second, early digital exposure tends to create long-term data exhaust. Once an address gets tied to ad-tech ecosystems, breach datasets, marketing lists, and low-quality signups, cleaning that up later is difficult. A private inbox does not eliminate all risk, but it does reduce how much unnecessary data collection gets normalized at the starting line.

Third, the campaign reflects a healthier security mindset. Good security is not only about locking down systems after the fact. It is also about making better default choices before the data starts flowing.

## Privacy for kids is not a niche concern

This part matters more than a lot of companies want to admit.

The FTC’s COPPA framework is built around the idea that parents should have control over the collection and use of personal information from children under 13. That is important, but compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. A company can meet legal requirements and still participate in an ecosystem that collects too much, retains too much, or normalizes tracking too early.

That is why I see value in privacy-first services even outside the compliance conversation. The question should not only be whether a company is allowed to collect a child’s data. The question should also be whether it needs to collect that much in the first place.

Proton’s broader privacy model is relevant here too. The company positions itself around no ads, no selling user data, and strong encryption protections, including zero-access encryption for stored data in its ecosystem. For families that are already uneasy with Big Tech’s business model, that difference is not just philosophical. It changes the trust model.

## What I like about this campaign

What I like most is that Born Private is not really pushing a fear message. It is pushing a boundary message.

Your child does not need to begin their digital life inside a system that treats personal data as inventory.

That is a message more privacy companies should be willing to say out loud.

I also like that the reserved address is not immediately active. That keeps the conversation where it should be: with the parent or guardian deciding when email makes sense, not with a platform nudging a child into earlier engagement.

## My take

I think Proton is right to frame this as a digital rights issue as much as a product feature.

A child’s first inbox should not be a data-harvesting on-ramp. It should be a controlled starting point. In a world where so much of the internet runs on profiling and retention, giving kids a private beginning is one of the few genuinely forward-thinking ideas I have seen from an email provider in a while.

Is reserving an email address for a child the most urgent security task every parent needs to tackle today? No. There are more immediate basics like strong passwords, multifactor authentication for family accounts, device safety, and good conversations around scams and online boundaries.

But as a principle, I think Born Private gets something important exactly right: privacy should be part of a child’s digital foundation, not an afterthought bolted on later.
