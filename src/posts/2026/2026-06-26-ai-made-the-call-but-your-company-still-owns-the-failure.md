> AI can make the recommendation, but it cannot sign the contract, accept the risk, or explain the failure to a regulator.

Imagine an AI-powered security platform receives an alert about unusual administrative activity.

The system analyzes the event, compares it against previous behavior, and decides it is probably a false positive. It automatically lowers the severity, suppresses similar alerts, and closes the case without sending it to a human analyst.

Two days later, the company discovers that the activity was part of a ransomware intrusion.

Who is responsible?

Is it the company that built the AI model? The security vendor that included it in its platform? The MSP that configured the system? The analyst who trusted the recommendation? Or the customer that approved automated alert closure in the first place?

The honest answer is that liability could reach several of them.

AI is moving beyond summarizing alerts and generating queries. I have written before about [where AI can fit into SOC workflows](/blog/putting-ai-to-work-in-the-soc/), but the risk changes once security products use it to prioritize incidents, recommend containment, identify malicious behavior, write remediation scripts, disable accounts, quarantine devices, and make other decisions that can materially affect an organization.

That creates an uncomfortable question the cybersecurity industry has not fully answered:

**When an AI system makes the wrong security decision, who owns the failure?**

*This article discusses general cybersecurity, governance, and liability considerations. It is not legal advice.*

## The AI is not going to be the defendant

The first thing to understand is that the AI itself is not likely to be the party held responsible.

It does not sign contracts. It does not purchase insurance. It does not approve risk. It does not have professional obligations to a client. It also cannot sit across from an executive team, regulator, insurer, or angry customer and explain why it missed an intrusion.

The responsibility remains with the people and organizations surrounding the technology.

That may include:

* The AI or security product vendor
* A foundation-model or threat-intelligence provider
* The MSP, MSSP, consultant, or systems integrator
* The organization deploying the system
* The employees approving or acting on its output
* Executives responsible for cybersecurity governance
* Other third parties involved in the service chain

The difficult part will be determining how responsibility should be divided.

> Shared responsibility does not mean equal responsibility.

## There is no AI exemption from existing law

The lack of one comprehensive federal AI liability law does not mean organizations can deploy AI without consequences.

The [Federal Trade Commission has stated that there is no AI exemption from existing laws](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes). Existing rules involving unfair or deceptive practices, privacy, data security, discrimination, contractual obligations, and professional negligence can still apply when AI is involved.

The FTC has already taken action against companies accused of overstating the effectiveness of AI products. Its case involving [Evolv Technologies focused on allegedly misleading claims about an AI-powered security screening system](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/11/ftc-takes-action-against-evolv-technologies-deceiving-users-about-its-ai-powered-security-screening). In another case, the agency challenged [unsupported accuracy claims made by an AI detection company](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/04/ftc-order-requires-workado-back-artificial-intelligence-detection-claims).

Those cases were not about an AI SOC platform missing ransomware, but the lesson transfers directly to cybersecurity vendors.

If a vendor claims its AI can autonomously detect threats, eliminate false positives, replace analyst review, or stop attacks before damage occurs, it needs evidence supporting those claims.

Calling something "AI-powered" does not lower the standard. In some cases, it may increase the expectations placed on the company selling it.

## Europe is beginning to define the responsibility chain

The European Union is taking a more explicit approach.

Where an AI system falls within the high-risk classifications of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the law establishes responsibilities for both providers and deployers.

Providers may be responsible for areas such as risk management, technical documentation, logging, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, and designing systems so they can be effectively overseen by humans.

Deployers also have responsibilities. They must use covered systems according to their instructions, assign oversight to people with appropriate competence and authority, monitor operation, and respond when risks or unexpected behavior appear.

This distinction matters because the company using an AI system cannot simply blame the vendor if it ignored documented limitations, removed safeguards, provided poor-quality input, or deployed the tool outside its intended purpose.

The EU's updated [Product Liability Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2853/oj/eng) also treats commercial software, including AI systems, as products for product-liability purposes. It recognizes that software updates, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, connected services, and a system's ability to continue learning may affect whether a product is considered defective.

However, it is not a blanket compensation mechanism for every cybersecurity incident. Its covered damages focus primarily on harm to natural persons, certain property damage, and destruction or corruption of data not used for professional purposes. Pure economic losses and business data may still need to be addressed through contracts, negligence claims, privacy law, or other national liability regimes.

The larger direction is still clear: AI is not being treated as an accountability escape hatch.

## Liability will follow control, knowledge, promises, and cause

After an AI-related cybersecurity failure, four questions will likely shape the investigation.

| Question                         | What it reveals                                                                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Who controlled the decision?     | Which party configured the system, granted permissions, selected the automation level, or approved the action                              |
| Who knew about the limitation?   | Whether known false-negative rates, unsupported environments, model weaknesses, or prior failures were disclosed or ignored                |
| What was promised?               | Whether marketing, contracts, SLAs, documentation, or sales statements overstated what the system could do                                 |
| What actually caused the damage? | Whether the harm resulted from a defective product, poor implementation, bad data, misuse, lack of oversight, or several failures together |

That is why the answer will rarely be as simple as "the vendor is liable" or "the customer accepted the risk."

Cybersecurity services are already built on shared responsibility. AI makes that relationship harder because one party may build the model, another may integrate it, an MSP may configure it, and the customer may decide how much authority it receives.

## When the vendor may be responsible

The AI or security vendor could face exposure when the failure comes from the design or operation of the product itself.

That could include:

* Materially misleading accuracy or performance claims
* Failure to disclose known limitations
* Insecure model interfaces or integrations
* A lack of reasonable logging or auditability
* Failure to address known vulnerabilities
* Unsafe default settings
* An AI system operating outside documented restrictions
* Updates that materially change behavior without adequate notice
* A system marketed for autonomous response that cannot safely perform that function

A vendor should not be able to advertise a platform as autonomous, intelligent, and capable of replacing human work, then retreat to "AI can make mistakes" after an incident.

Warnings and disclaimers matter, but they should not erase the core promises used to sell the product.

## When the MSP, MSSP, or integrator may be responsible

This is where the issue becomes especially important for managed service providers.

An MSP may not have created the model, but it may have selected the product, configured the integrations, written the response playbooks, set the thresholds, granted permissions, and sold the resulting service to the customer.

That is part of why [AI exposes operational debt inside MSP security stacks](/blog/ai-is-exposing-the-operational-debt-inside-msp-security-stacks/). Weak ownership, unclear escalation paths, and untested response workflows become much harder to defend once an AI system is making or accelerating decisions.

If the MSP markets the service as 24/7 AI-powered detection and response, the customer is not going to begin its complaint with the model developer. The customer is going to contact the MSP.

Exposure could increase when the provider:

* Enables automated containment without adequate testing
* Fails to follow the vendor's deployment guidance
* Does not validate the system against the customer's environment
* Removes human review to reduce staffing costs
* Ignores recurring false positives or false negatives
* Gives the tool unnecessary administrative privileges
* Fails to notify the customer about known limitations
* Does not preserve the logs needed to investigate a failure
* Promises an outcome that is not supported by the underlying service

The service agreement will matter, but so will the MSP's actual conduct.

A limitation-of-liability clause will not make poor implementation disappear. It may limit damages, depending on the contract and applicable law, but it does not change what happened.

## When the customer may be responsible

The organization deploying the system also owns part of the risk.

A customer may increase its exposure if it disables recommended telemetry, refuses required security controls, ignores warnings, uses the system outside its intended purpose, or grants autonomous authority without appropriate review.

The organization also decides whether the AI is advisory or operational.

There is a major difference between asking an AI system to summarize an alert and allowing it to:

* Disable a user account
* Revoke sessions
* Isolate an endpoint
* Block network traffic
* Delete a file
* Modify firewall rules
* Rotate credentials
* Execute a generated script

Once an organization gives the system authority to change the environment, the organization has made a governance decision, not merely a technology purchase.

That decision should be documented, tested, approved, and reviewed like any other material security control.

## "Human in the loop" is not automatically a defense

Many vendors and organizations point to human oversight as the answer.

That is important, but the phrase is becoming almost meaningless.

A human is not providing meaningful oversight when they receive hundreds of AI-generated recommendations and are expected to approve each one within seconds. That is not review. It is a rubber stamp with a username attached to it.

Meaningful oversight requires the person to have:

* Enough information to understand the recommendation
* Access to the supporting evidence
* Time to challenge the conclusion
* Training on the system's limitations
* Authority to reject or reverse the action
* A reliable way to stop the system
* Protection from being punished for slowing down automation when something looks wrong

> Human oversight is not a control when the human cannot realistically investigate the decision.

Organizations should also watch for automation bias. Once a tool develops a reputation for being correct, analysts may stop independently evaluating its conclusions.

That creates a dangerous feedback loop. The AI becomes trusted because people rarely challenge it, and people rarely challenge it because it is considered trusted.

## How different failures could distribute responsibility

The exact answer will depend on the facts, contracts, jurisdiction, and damage involved. Still, several common scenarios show how responsibility could be divided.

### The AI misses an intrusion

The vendor may face questions if it made unsupported detection claims or concealed known limitations.

The MSP or MSSP may face questions if it promised continuous monitoring but allowed the AI to close alerts without meaningful analyst review.

The customer may share responsibility if it disabled telemetry, rejected recommended services, or ignored repeated warnings.

### The AI causes an outage during containment

The integrator or deploying organization may be responsible if it granted excessive permissions, skipped testing, or failed to create rollback procedures.

The vendor may carry more responsibility if the product ignored configured restrictions or performed an action outside its documented behavior.

### An AI-generated remediation script deletes data

The organization executing an untested script may own much of the immediate failure, especially if there was no backup or change-control process.

The vendor's responsibility may increase if the system was specifically marketed as safe for autonomous code execution and the failure resulted from a product defect rather than an unpredictable output that required review.

### Sensitive security data is exposed to an external model

The customer, MSP, or employee may be responsible for sending confidential logs, credentials, client information, or incident data to an unapproved service.

That is the same visibility problem behind [shadow AI becoming the new shadow IT](/blog/shadow-ai-is-the-new-shadow-it-but-blocking-it-wont-work/): useful tools appear faster than the organization's review, data, identity, and logging controls.

The model provider may face responsibility if it violated its stated retention, confidentiality, security, or training commitments.

In each situation, several failures can exist at the same time.

## The contract will decide the first fight

Before anyone reaches a courtroom, the parties will probably reach for their contracts.

Organizations purchasing or reselling AI-powered security products should review more than price, uptime, and license counts.

The agreement should clearly address:

* Which functions use AI
* Whether AI decisions are advisory or autonomous
* The documented performance limitations
* Required human-review points
* Data retention and model-training practices
* Ownership of prompts, outputs, logs, and incident data
* Notification requirements after security or model failures
* Access to evidence needed for an investigation
* Changes to the underlying model or service
* Responsibility for third-party models and data sources
* Indemnification and liability limitations
* Cyber, technology errors and omissions, and professional-liability coverage

A traditional uptime SLA may be nearly useless in this situation.

The platform may have been available 99.99 percent of the time while making the wrong decision during every minute that mattered.

Decision quality, logging, response time, escalation, false negatives, and the ability to recover from automated actions may be more important than basic availability.

## A defensible AI security program needs evidence

The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) is voluntary, but it provides a useful structure for governing, mapping, measuring, and managing AI risks. NIST is also developing a [Cyber AI Profile](https://www.nccoe.nist.gov/projects/cyber-ai-profile) focused on securing AI systems, using AI for cyber defense, and defending against AI-enabled attacks.

This is also where the broader GRC muscle matters. If you already have [one risk-based governance program that maps controls to multiple frameworks](/blog/grc-in-the-real-world-making-hipaa-pci-nist-csf-ftc-safeguards-and-nis2-work-together/), AI accountability becomes another risk scenario to govern instead of a completely separate paperwork universe.

Following a framework does not guarantee immunity from liability.

It does, however, help an organization demonstrate that it identified risks, assigned ownership, evaluated the system, and implemented controls instead of blindly deploying whatever the vendor labeled as intelligent.

Organizations using AI in security operations should be able to produce evidence showing:

1. **What the system was allowed to do.** Maintain an inventory of AI capabilities, integrations, privileges, and autonomous actions.

2. **Who approved the authority.** Document who accepted the risk and why the selected automation level was appropriate.

3. **How it was tested.** Validate the system against the organization's actual environment, not only the vendor's demonstration data.

4. **What information informed each decision.** Preserve relevant prompts, model versions, telemetry, recommendations, tool actions, approvals, and overrides.

5. **How humans can intervene.** Define clear escalation paths, approval thresholds, stop mechanisms, and emergency shutdown procedures.

6. **How actions can be reversed.** Maintain backups, rollback processes, manual alternatives, and tested recovery procedures.

7. **How performance is monitored.** Track false positives, false negatives, drift, unexplained decisions, failed actions, and analyst overrides.

8. **How incidents involving AI will be handled.** Update incident-response plans so the organization knows how to preserve evidence, disable automation, notify affected parties, and investigate the model's role.

For MSPs, documentation should also show which controls the client accepted, declined, or changed.

That documentation should not be treated as a way to blame the customer. It is a way to make the responsibility boundary clear before an incident forces everyone to argue about it.

## Do not outsource accountability with the workload

AI can help overwhelmed security teams process more data, investigate incidents faster, and respond at a scale humans cannot manage alone.

That does not mean it should operate without boundaries.

The more authority an organization delegates to an AI system, the more attention it must give to testing, logging, oversight, contracts, recovery, and accountability.

That is especially true for agentic systems. Once AI can use tools, call APIs, and take action across business workflows, [it becomes an identity, access, and accountability problem](/blog/agentic-ai-is-securitys-next-blind-spot-because-it-can-act/), not just another chatbot risk.

The companies that run into the most trouble will probably not be the ones that used AI.

They will be the ones that could not explain:

* Why they trusted it
* What it was allowed to do
* Who approved it
* Whether anyone monitored it
* How they tested it
* What happened when it failed

AI can automate a security decision. It cannot own the consequences.

**Do not give an AI system more authority than your organization can audit, explain, and reverse.**
