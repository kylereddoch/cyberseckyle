IT and cybersecurity hiring has a gatekeeping problem.

I do not mean that every requirement is unfair. Some jobs really do need someone who has touched the system before. If a company needs a senior identity engineer, a cloud security architect, or someone who can lead incident response during a live breach, it is reasonable to ask for specific experience. Nobody wants a person learning the basics of firewall policy, privilege escalation, or endpoint containment for the first time while production is actively on fire.

That is not the part that bothers me.

The part that bothers me is how often the industry talks about needing more people, then builds hiring filters that only reward people who already had access to the exact tools, titles, and environments the job is supposed to train them into.

That is where the gatekeeping starts.

A degree in IT, cybersecurity, computer science, information systems, or a related field should mean something. It does not mean the person is automatically ready for every technical job. It does not replace hands-on experience. It does not prove judgment under pressure. But it does show that the person can stick with a structured learning path, absorb unfamiliar concepts, write, troubleshoot, research, meet deadlines, and build enough foundation to keep learning.

That should matter in a field where the work changes constantly.

Instead, a lot of candidates find out that the degree is treated like a checkbox at best. Employers may say they want problem solvers, learners, and people with potential, but the actual posting asks for a very specific stack: three years of Azure, two years of Sentinel, CrowdStrike experience, Okta, Palo Alto, Python, PowerShell, vulnerability management, incident response, compliance, networking, Linux, Windows Server, and a certification or two. Then the job is labeled entry-level or junior because the salary band says it is.

That mismatch is brutal.

## The degree is not worthless, but it is not enough by itself

This is the part that needs some honesty on both sides.

Job seekers are not wrong to feel frustrated when they spent years earning a degree and still get screened out for not having one narrow tool on their resume. That degree likely required networking, operating systems, databases, programming, security fundamentals, project work, writing, and plenty of learning under pressure. Those are real signals.

But employers are also not completely wrong to want evidence that someone can do the job in the environment they actually run.

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/information-security-analysts.htm) describes information security analysts as typically needing a bachelor's degree in a computer science field, related work experience, and sometimes professional certification. BLS also projects 29% growth for information security analyst roles from 2024 to 2034, which is much faster than the average occupation. So the demand story is real.

The problem is that "demand" does not automatically create clean on-ramps.

Cybersecurity is especially awkward because many security jobs are not truly entry-level. A SOC analyst needs enough networking knowledge to know what looks weird. A vulnerability analyst needs enough systems context to understand why a patch is risky or urgent. A GRC analyst needs enough business and technical awareness to know whether a control is meaningful or theater. Even junior security work benefits from previous IT exposure.

That reality should shape training paths, internships, apprenticeships, and job design.

It should not become an excuse to call a job "entry-level" while asking for someone who already sounds mid-career.

## Skills-based hiring sounds good until it becomes keyword-based hiring

There is a difference between skills-based hiring and keyword-based hiring.

Skills-based hiring asks, "Can this person do the work, learn the missing pieces, and grow into the role?"

Keyword-based hiring asks, "Did this person use the exact nouns in our job description?"

Those are not the same thing.

The [Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School](https://www.burningglassinstitute.org/research/skills-based-hiring-2024) have pointed out that a lot of employers announced a move away from degree requirements, but the actual hiring change has been slower and less consistent than the headlines made it sound. That tracks with what many job seekers feel. The paper ceiling may move in one place while another wall gets built out of tools, certifications, years of experience, and applicant tracking filters.

In cybersecurity, that can get weird fast.

A candidate might understand SIEM concepts, log sources, alert triage, basic detection logic, and incident documentation, but get passed over because they used Splunk in a lab and the job wants Microsoft Sentinel in production. Another candidate might understand identity fundamentals, MFA, conditional access concepts, least privilege, and account recovery risk, but get filtered out because they have not administered Okta specifically. Someone else might have years of help desk, endpoint, and scripting experience, but lose to a resume parser because their title was not "security analyst."

That is not skills-based hiring.

That is tool-name matching.

Tools matter. Production experience matters. But if the hiring process cannot tell the difference between a missing tool name and a missing skill, it is going to reject people who could have become strong employees with a reasonable ramp.

## Entry-level should not mean "already validated by someone else"

The entry-level cyber problem is often an ownership problem.

Everyone wants entry-level talent that someone else already trained.

Colleges are expected to produce job-ready graduates. Bootcamps are expected to compress years of experience into months. Certification vendors are expected to prove competence with exams. Home labs are expected to stand in for production exposure. And when all of that still does not feel like enough, employers ask for prior experience anyway.

That creates a ridiculous loop:

1. You need experience to get the job.
2. You need the job to get experience.
3. You try to build proof outside the job.
4. Employers say the proof is not the same as real experience.
5. You are back at step one.

This is where the frustration turns into something unhealthy.

The [ISC2 2025 Cybersecurity Hiring Trends Report](https://www.isc2.org/Insights/2025/06/cybersecurity-hiring-trends-study) is useful because it says the quiet part out loud. ISC2 noted that more than a third of hiring managers wanted advanced certifications or unrealistic skills for entry and junior-level candidates. The same report also points to internships and apprenticeships as important sources for early-career talent, and says some hiring managers are looking beyond traditional cybersecurity and computer science backgrounds.

That is the direction we need more of.

If an organization wants junior people, it has to build junior roles. That means tighter scope, clearer expectations, better mentorship, documented workflows, and a realistic training period. It means being honest about what must be known on day one and what can be learned in the first 30, 60, or 90 days.

If the team cannot support that, then it should stop calling the role entry-level.

## The resume pressure is a symptom, not just a character flaw

I am not going to tell people to lie on resumes.

Do not do that.

It can burn your reputation, fail a background check, put you into a job you cannot actually perform, and make every future interview harder. In cybersecurity especially, trust matters. If the first signal you send is that you are willing to misrepresent access, experience, or credentials, that is a bad foundation.

But I also think it is too easy to moralize about resume dishonesty without looking at the system that creates the pressure.

Monster's 2026 [Credibility Gap Report](https://www.monster.com/career-advice/research/credibility-gap) found that 13% of U.S. job seekers admitted to recently lying or including misleading information on a resume. Among those who misrepresented something, the most common areas included employment dates, responsibilities, skills or tool proficiency, job titles, results, education credentials, and certifications.

That does not make lying acceptable.

It does show what people feel pressured to stretch.

If every posting demands exact experience, candidates start translating their background into the language the system rewards. Some do that ethically by reframing transferable skills. Some cross the line. The more rigid and unrealistic the filter becomes, the more tempting it is for a desperate person to write the resume the system seems to want instead of the resume that honestly describes what they have done.

Again, that is not a defense of lying.

It is a warning sign.

Hiring systems teach candidates what they believe matters. If the lesson is "only exact keywords survive," candidates will optimize for exact keywords. If the lesson is "we value demonstrated learning, adjacent experience, labs, writing, troubleshooting, and honest growth," candidates have a better path to show what they can actually do.

## Gatekeeping hides behind risk

Some gatekeeping is obvious.

"You need a degree from this kind of school."

"You need five years of experience for a junior role."

"You need a CISSP before you have even had enough professional experience to hold the certification cleanly."

That stuff is easy to criticize.

The harder version hides behind risk.

Hiring managers are scared of making a bad hire. Security teams are already overloaded. Nobody wants to spend months training someone who leaves. Nobody wants to hire a person who cannot handle the work. Nobody wants to explain to leadership why the new analyst missed something important.

Those concerns are real.

But risk cuts both ways.

There is risk in hiring someone inexperienced and unsupported. There is also risk in never building a pipeline. There is risk in filtering for the same narrow profile over and over. There is risk in understaffing teams because nobody matches the fantasy posting. There is risk in burning out the few people who do meet the bar because the organization never figured out how to grow the next layer.

The [CyberSeek heat map](https://www.cyberseek.org/heatmap.html) continues to show hundreds of thousands of cybersecurity-related job postings in the United States, while also tracking supply and demand across roles, regions, certifications, and the NICE Framework categories. That data is a reminder that the problem is not only whether people are interested in cybersecurity. The problem is matching real people to real work in a way that does not collapse into wish lists.

Gatekeeping feels safe because it narrows the funnel.

Sometimes it just narrows the imagination.

## Better job descriptions would separate foundations from preferences

One practical fix is simple, but not easy: write job descriptions like someone actually understands the job.

Separate requirements into three groups:

- What the person must know on day one
- What they can learn with support
- What would be nice but should not decide the whole search

For example, a junior SOC role might require basic networking, Windows and Linux familiarity, security fundamentals, documentation discipline, and the ability to explain an alert investigation clearly. Experience with a specific SIEM can be a preference. Experience with the exact EDR can be trainable. Prior production incident response can be a bonus instead of a hard gate.

That is not lowering the bar.

That is naming the bar correctly.

NIST's [NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice/nice-framework-resource-center) exists for exactly this kind of clarity. It gives employers, educators, and workers a common language for cybersecurity work, knowledge, skills, job descriptions, and candidate assessment. That kind of framework is useful because it pushes the conversation away from vague titles and toward actual tasks.

If a posting says "security analyst," that can mean almost anything.

If it says the person will triage phishing reports, review endpoint detections, escalate suspicious identity events, document timelines, update tickets, and follow playbooks, then candidates and hiring teams both get a clearer picture.

Clarity is more humane.

It is also better security.

## Job seekers need a better way to show transferable skill

This is not only on employers.

Job seekers also need to learn how to translate their experience without inflating it.

If your degree gave you networking, Linux, scripting, risk, policy, or database experience, do not just list the degree and hope someone infers the rest. Pull the useful parts forward. If you built a lab, write down what you built, what broke, what you learned, and how you validated it. If you worked help desk, connect that to identity, endpoint troubleshooting, ticket discipline, user communication, escalation, and root-cause thinking.

Do not say you managed a SIEM if you watched a few videos.

Do say you built a home lab that ingested Windows event logs, wrote basic detection queries, investigated failed login patterns, and documented findings.

Do not say you administered enterprise Azure if you only used a student tenant.

Do say you configured a test tenant, created users and groups, tested MFA policies, reviewed sign-in logs, and learned how conditional access decisions are evaluated.

That distinction matters.

The goal is not to make your experience sound bigger than it was. The goal is to make the learning visible enough that a reasonable employer can see the bridge between where you are and where the role needs you to go.

## Employers should hire for learning velocity, not only inventory

The uncomfortable truth is that nobody in IT or cybersecurity is fully current forever.

Tools change. Licensing changes. Threats change. Cloud platforms change. Attackers change. Business priorities change. Compliance expectations change. Even the same tool can behave differently depending on how the environment is designed.

So yes, hire for skills.

But do not confuse a static inventory of tools with long-term ability.

A good candidate can learn, communicate, troubleshoot, document, ask useful questions, admit uncertainty, and keep going when the answer is not obvious. A good candidate can connect a technical problem to operational impact. A good candidate knows when to escalate, when to research, and when to stop guessing.

Those traits are harder to measure than "two years of CrowdStrike."

They are also the traits that decide whether someone keeps growing after the hire.

Degrees, certifications, labs, previous jobs, portfolios, and interviews are all just signals. None of them are perfect. The hiring process should be trying to combine those signals into a picture of capability and growth, not using each one as a separate gate to slam shut.

## The industry cannot complain about the pipeline while blocking the doors

Cybersecurity keeps saying it needs people.

IT keeps saying it needs people.

Businesses keep saying technology risk is growing.

Then too many postings ask for the already-formed version of the employee they never invested in creating.

That is the contradiction.

If we want better hiring, we need to stop pretending the only safe candidate is the one who already did the job somewhere else. We need more honest job descriptions, better apprenticeships and internships, stronger mentorship, clearer junior roles, and interviews that test reasoning instead of memorized tool trivia. We need to respect degrees as one signal of learning without pretending they replace practical work. We need to respect practical experience without turning every tool name into a wall.

Most of all, we need to stop treating potential like a liability.

Potential is not enough by itself. It needs structure, support, and accountability.

But without it, the field just keeps recycling the same experienced people until they burn out, leave, or become the next impossible requirement on a job posting.

That is not a talent strategy.

That is gatekeeping with a staffing problem attached.
