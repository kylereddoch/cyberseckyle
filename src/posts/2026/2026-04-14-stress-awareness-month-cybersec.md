> In cybersecurity and IT, stress gets normalized way too easily. That does not make it healthy, and it definitely does not make it sustainable.

April is [Stress Awareness Month](https://stressawarenessmonth.com/), and I think that is a good excuse for those of us in cybersecurity and IT to be honest about something our industry still handles poorly. We are very good at talking about uptime, resilience, and risk. We are not always as good at talking about the human cost of constantly operating in that mode.

In this line of work, stress often gets framed like proof that you care. If you are answering messages after hours, jumping into every emergency, chasing every alert, and carrying the weight of every missed patch or misconfiguration, it can start to look like commitment. In reality, it often looks a lot more like slow-motion burnout.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) defines burnout as a syndrome that results from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is marked by exhaustion, increased mental distance or cynicism toward the job, and reduced professional effectiveness. That definition matters because it shifts the conversation. Burnout is not just being tired after a long week. It is what happens when the work keeps taking more than the person can realistically keep giving.

That hits close to home in cybersecurity and IT. We work in environments where the demands are often nonstop. There are tickets, outages, phishing attempts, late-night maintenance windows, vulnerability remediation cycles, compliance deadlines, frustrated users, and the constant feeling that one missed detail could turn into a real business problem. The [CDC’s National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/stress/about/index.html) says job stress happens when work requirements do not match the capabilities, resources, or needs of the worker. That description fits a lot of IT and cyber roles a little too well.

The pressure is not just anecdotal either. [ISACA’s 2024 State of Cybersecurity research](https://www.isaca.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/2024/nearly-two-thirds-of-cybersecurity-pros-say-job-stress-is-growing-according-to-new-isaca-research) found that 66% of cybersecurity professionals say their role is more stressful now than it was five years ago. The top reasons included a more complex threat landscape, low budgets, hiring and retention problems, and insufficiently trained staff. Then [ISACA’s 2025 data](https://www.isaca.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/2025/state-of-cybersecurity-2025-global-press-release) showed that more than half of cybersecurity teams are understaffed, 65% have unfilled positions, and half of organizations struggle to retain cyber talent. None of that surprises me. A lot of teams are trying to defend more systems, more identities, more data, and more business processes without enough time or enough people.

That is why I think balance in this field needs to be redefined. Balance does not mean pretending the work is not serious. It does not mean ignoring incidents or acting like security risks can wait. It means building a way of working that does not require people to stay in a constant state of depletion just to keep the lights on.

The [U.S. Surgeon General’s Framework for Workplace Mental Health and Well-Being](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/workplace-well-being/index.html) gets this right. It points to things like psychological safety, adequate rest, work-life harmony, and respect for boundaries between work and non-work time. Those ideas may sound soft to people who are used to high-pressure technical environments, but I would argue they are actually operational issues. Tired people miss things. Burned-out people disengage. Teams that are always stretched thin stop improving and start surviving.

So what does moving from burnout to balance actually look like in cybersecurity and IT?

For one, it means stopping the habit of building teams around heroics. If the environment only works because one person always answers after hours, remembers every undocumented dependency, and mentally carries the whole system map, that is not resilience. That is fragility with a hard-working face on it. Better documentation, better runbooks, cleaner escalation paths, and shared ownership do more for long-term stability than constant firefighting ever will.

It also means respecting boundaries before people are already running on empty. The Surgeon General’s workplace framework specifically calls out adequate rest and protecting the line between work and non-work time. In practical terms, that can mean rotating on-call fairly, being honest about what is truly urgent, and not turning every inconvenience into an emergency. In a lot of IT shops, especially MSP and support-heavy environments, the line between responsiveness and constant availability gets blurry fast. That blur is where a lot of burnout starts.

Another part of balance is reducing unnecessary cognitive load. Not every problem in cybersecurity can be eliminated, but a lot of friction can be reduced. Noisy alerting, poor asset visibility, unclear ownership, weak documentation, and tool sprawl all create stress that has nothing to do with actual security outcomes. If leaders want healthier teams, one of the smartest things they can do is simplify what can be simplified. Fewer avoidable distractions means more room for people to do thoughtful work instead of reacting all day.

I also think we need to get better at admitting when stress is already affecting us. The warning signs are not always dramatic at first. Sometimes it is irritability. Sometimes it is difficulty concentrating. Sometimes it is that feeling of being mentally "done" before the day even really starts. Sometimes it is cynicism toward users, teammates, or the work itself. The [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health) says it is worth seeking professional help when symptoms such as sleep problems, trouble concentrating, loss of interest, irritability, or difficulty handling normal responsibilities last for two weeks or more. That should not carry stigma. Taking mental health seriously is not weakness. It is maintenance, just like patching a server before it fails.

Stress Awareness Month is a reminder that awareness alone is not enough. Cybersecurity and IT do not need more slogans about wellness while the workload stays broken. We need healthier team design, better staffing decisions, clearer boundaries, stronger documentation, smarter prioritization, and leadership that understands people are not infinitely elastic.

I love this field, but I do not think it should demand burnout as the price of entry. The best teams are not the ones that can suffer silently the longest. They are the ones that can do meaningful, high-stakes work without destroying the people doing it.

That is the kind of balance worth building.
