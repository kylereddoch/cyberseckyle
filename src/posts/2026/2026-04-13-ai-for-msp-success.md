There is a version of the AI conversation in managed services that already feels worn out.

It is the version where every vendor demo implies the future MSP is almost autonomous, every workflow is one prompt away from solving itself, and every owner just needs to "embrace AI" before the competition leaves them behind. I do not buy that version of the story. From where I sit, AI is not a replacement for a good MSP. It is a multiplier for the MSP that already knows how to standardize, document, secure, and communicate well. The firms that are going to get real value from AI are not the ones making the loudest noise about it. They are the ones using it to reduce internal drag, tighten service delivery, and free their people up for higher-value work. That is part of why I wanted to include the recent [MSP Success piece on five AI trends defining success for MSPs in 2026](https://mspsuccess.com/2026/03/5-ai-trends-defining-success-for-msps-in-2026/). It makes a point I think a lot of providers need to hear: the MSPs getting traction with AI did not start with the sales pitch. They started by using it on themselves first.

## The first win is usually internal

That feels right to me.

In an MSP, there is no shortage of work that has to get done but does not really move the needle in a meaningful way. Ticket summarization, documentation cleanup, internal note-taking, report prep, recurring service updates, and triage are all necessary, but they are also the kinds of tasks that slowly drain technician time and attention. AI starts to matter when it helps remove some of that drag. The same pattern shows up in [Kaseya's 2025 Global MSP Benchmark coverage](https://www.kaseya.com/blog/key-findings-from-kaseyas-2025-global-msp-benchmark-report/) and in its more recent commentary on [how AI can help MSPs scale through unified tools](https://www.kaseya.com/blog/ai-msp-scale-through-unified-tools/). The message is not that AI replaces the team. It is that it can help eliminate tedious work, create more room for strategy, and make the stack more usable when it is tied together properly.

That last part matters more than the AI feature list.

If your ticket data is inconsistent, your documentation is stale, your asset information is incomplete, and your tools are all maintaining their own slightly different version of reality, AI is not going to fix that. It is going to amplify it. Kaseya's benchmarking also highlights how strongly MSPs value integration between RMM, PSA, and documentation systems, and I think that tells the real story. AI is not most valuable in chaos. It is most valuable in an environment that already has some structure. The MSPs that benefit first are the ones that have done the boring work of standardization.

## AI will reward mature MSPs, not messy ones

This is probably my strongest opinion on the subject.

AI is not going to save an undisciplined MSP. It is going to reward mature ones.

If you already have standardized processes, a sane stack, decent onboarding, good escalation paths, and documentation that technicians can actually trust, AI has something useful to build on. If you do not, it becomes another layer of noise on top of an already noisy operation. That is why I think some providers are approaching this backward. They want the AI capability before they have the operating model that makes AI worth using. In practice, the order should be the opposite. Build a cleaner service engine first. Then use AI to sharpen it.

That same idea shows up in [ConnectWise's take on AI and hyperautomation in the MSP industry](https://www.connectwise.com/blog/current-impact-of-ai-and-hyperautomation-on-the-msp-industry). Strip away the vendor angle and the underlying point is still solid: repeatable service delivery, tech stack discipline, and process maturity are what create room for automation to work well. AI likes structure. It does not magically create it.

## Clients are not just buying AI tools

I also think the opportunity here is bigger than reselling licenses or bolting a chatbot onto an offering and calling it innovation.

Most clients are somewhere between curious and overwhelmed. They are hearing about AI from every direction at once, but many of them still do not know where it actually fits, what it is likely to improve, or where the risks begin. That is where MSPs have a real opening, but only if they are willing to act like advisors instead of product pushers. According to [Kaseya's benchmark findings](https://www.kaseya.com/blog/key-findings-from-kaseyas-2025-global-msp-benchmark-report/), clients are still heavily focused on security and many want guidance on best practices, not just tools. That feels exactly right to me. The value is not in saying, "Here is an AI product." The value is in saying, "Here is where AI could make sense in your business, here is where it could go wrong, and here is how we would approach it without making a mess."

That broader advisory angle also shows up outside the traditional channel space. In its [2026 Managed Services Outlook](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/our-insights/transformation/managed-services-outlook.html), KPMG frames managed services as a strategic priority for nearly all surveyed organizations and points to AI management and cybersecurity as leading investment priorities. That is an important combination. Not AI by itself. AI management and cybersecurity together. That is a much more grounded framing of what businesses are actually asking for. They do not just want access to AI. They want help using it responsibly, securely, and in ways that actually improve operations.

## The real danger is AI theater

A lot of AI activity right now still feels like theater.

The demo looks good. The proof of concept gets everybody excited. The meeting energy is high. Then the whole thing drifts, stalls, or dies quietly because nobody did the hard work underneath it. That is why the phrase [AI pilot purgatory](https://mspsuccess.com/2026/03/escaping-ai-pilot-purgatory/) lands so well. The common failure pattern is not hard to recognize. Sample data looks clean. Real business data does not. Nobody clearly owns the business outcome. Nobody defines what success actually looks like. Nobody does the prep work around governance, access, or data readiness. Then the pilot becomes a stalled project and everyone blames the technology.

I do not think that is mostly a technology problem. I think it is usually a scoping and operational problem.

For MSPs, that has real implications for how AI services should be packaged. The better motion is not, "Let's deploy something flashy and see what happens." The better motion is to start with the workflow, the business problem, the data sources, the ownership model, the security controls, and the success metric. Then decide where AI belongs. That is a much more serious offering, and I think clients are going to trust it a lot more than a vague promise that AI will somehow transform everything.

## Security has to be in the middle of the conversation

This is the part that matters most to me.

AI adoption is not only a productivity discussion. It is also an identity, governance, access, logging, and data exposure discussion. If an MSP is going to help clients move into AI tools, assistants, copilots, or workflow agents, it also needs to be ready to talk about who can access what, what data those systems are touching, how usage is monitored, and where the controls are. Otherwise, the provider is not really leading the client. It is just introducing new attack surface.

That is why I think the guidance coming out of places like [NIST's draft Cyber AI Profile](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2025/12/draft-nist-guidelines-rethink-cybersecurity-ai-era) is useful. NIST frames the issue across three connected areas: securing AI systems, using AI to improve defense, and dealing with AI-enabled attacks. That is a balanced way to think about it. AI is not just something you use. It is also something you have to secure and something adversaries will use too.

Microsoft is making a similar point in its security guidance for the [agentic AI era](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/03/20/secure-agentic-ai-end-to-end/). The emphasis there is on visibility, identity, sensitive data protection, and Zero Trust thinking applied to AI workflows. For MSPs, that should be a signal. If the service conversation around AI does not include identity and governance, it is incomplete.

## AI in security operations is already practical

There is also a defensive side to this that goes beyond note summarization and content generation.

AI is already pushing further into investigation, triage, and response workflows. That is one reason the conversation feels different now than it did even a year or two ago. For example, [N-able's recent SOC-related reporting](https://www.n-able.com/press/press-releases/n-able-report-reveals-why-ai-powered-layered-cyber-defense-is-essential-for-business-resilience) puts heavy emphasis on autonomous investigation activity and orchestrated alert handling. I would not treat any single vendor statistic as a universal truth, but I do think the larger direction is clear. AI is becoming part of real defensive operations, not just office productivity.

For MSPs, that means the opportunity is broader than client-facing copilots or internal efficiency gains. It also means thinking carefully about where AI can accelerate security work without removing the human judgment that still matters. Used well, it can help teams move faster. Used badly, it can make people trust automation they do not fully understand.

## Better efficiency should not mean cheaper service

I also think some MSPs are going to make a mistake by treating AI-driven efficiency like it should automatically turn into lower prices.

That sounds fine until you think about what is actually being improved. If AI helps reduce wasted time, improve ticket flow, clean up reporting, or strengthen consistency, the goal should not be to immediately race toward cheaper service. The goal should be to turn those gains into a better client experience and stronger margins. [ConnectWise's hyperautomation analysis](https://www.connectwise.com/blog/current-impact-of-ai-and-hyperautomation-on-the-msp-industry) makes that case pretty clearly. Aggressive price-cutting in response to efficiency gains can hurt the business faster than people expect. Best-in-class MSPs tend to protect value instead of rushing to commoditize themselves.

I think that is dead on.

If AI helps an MSP become more efficient, that does not mean the work suddenly matters less. It means the MSP has more room to respond faster, communicate better, document more clearly, and spend human time where it counts most. That is value. That should not be thrown away just because a vendor slide deck said automation saves labor.

## My take

I do think AI is going to matter in the next phase of the MSP market.

I just do not think it is going to matter in the lazy, overhyped way a lot of vendors want to frame it.

The MSPs that pull ahead will use AI to reduce internal drag, sharpen service delivery, strengthen security operations, and guide clients through decisions that are still messy and uncertain. They will not treat AI like magic. They will treat it like leverage. They will care about documentation, integration, governance, identity, and measurable outcomes. They will understand that trust is still the product, even when automation gets better.

That is the part I keep coming back to.

Clients are still buying judgment. They are still buying consistency. They are still buying responsiveness and confidence and the sense that somebody competent is paying attention. AI can absolutely help an MSP deliver more of that. But it cannot replace it. And the provider that forgets that is probably going to end up with more tools, more noise, and less actual value.
