> I have been getting versions of this question for a while now on Mastodon and elsewhere, so I figured it was finally worth answering in one place.

Every time a major cybersecurity story breaks, there is a rush.

A new breach. A new zero-day. A new vendor statement. A new round of panic. A new thread from people trying to explain what it all means before the dust has even settled.

And somewhere in the middle of that cycle, I will usually get a question that sounds something like this:

## “Why haven’t you written about this yet?”

That is a fair question.

If you follow my work, you know I write a lot about cybersecurity, IT, risk, privacy, business technology, and the real-world side of managing and defending systems. So when a big story hits and I do not immediately publish something, it can probably look odd from the outside. Especially in a space where everybody seems expected to have an instant take.

The truth is, I do not try to cover every security story the moment it lands.

That is not because I do not care. It is not because I missed it. It is not because I have nothing to say.

It is because I know what kind of work I want this site to do, and I know what kind of writing I do not want to become.

This site is not built to be a breaking news wire. It is built to be useful.

## What I am actually trying to do here

There are already plenty of people and outlets doing excellent work covering security news minute by minute. We need that. Fast reporting matters. Timely analysis matters. Technical researchers publishing rapid findings matter.

But that is not the lane I am trying to live in every day.

My posts tend to be a mix of strategic analysis, practitioner insight, tutorials, industry perspective, and the kind of commentary that comes from actually working in and around these problems. I care a lot more about whether a post helps someone understand the *why* behind a story than whether I can be one of the first people to say it happened.

That difference matters.

A fast take can be useful in the moment. A thoughtful take can still be useful six months later.

I would rather publish something that helps a reader think better, make a stronger decision, improve a process, harden an environment, or understand the broader pattern than publish a rushed post just so I can say I covered the headline.

That is the short version.

The longer version is probably best answered through the kinds of questions I tend to get.

## Questions I get, and my honest answers

### “Do you just skip stories if you are late to them?”

No.

I just do not think “late” is the same thing as “irrelevant.”

A lot of security writing gets trapped in the idea that the first take is the only take that matters. I think that mindset is wrong. In fact, some stories are *better* understood a few days later, once the first wave of noise, speculation, vendor damage control, and social media hot takes have had time to settle.

There is a huge difference between reacting to an alert and understanding an incident.

Sometimes waiting lets you see what actually matters:

- what changed
- what the real risk was
- who is affected
- what defenders should realistically do
- what the story says about the bigger direction of the industry

That is usually the part I care about most.

### “So are you not trying to be a cybersecurity news account?”

Correct. That has never really been the goal.

I talk about security news when I think I have something meaningful to add. But I am not trying to become a constant-feed news machine that posts every time a vendor sneezes, a researcher drops a thread, or social media decides this week’s panic is the end of civilization.

There is already enough noise in cybersecurity.

I do not want to add to it unless I can add signal too.

### “But wouldn’t posting more breaking news help grow your audience?”

Maybe.

It would probably give me more opportunities to jump into trending conversations. It would probably make the content cadence look busier. It might even pull in some extra traffic from people searching for the latest headline.

But not all growth is good growth.

I would rather build an audience that knows what to expect from me than chase short-term clicks from whatever topic is on fire that afternoon. I want people to come here because they trust the perspective, not because I am trying to win the race to repeat the same facts everybody else already posted.

There is a big difference between being visible and being valuable.

I care more about the second one.

### “Do you avoid stories because you do not want to be wrong?”

I think more people in this space should be comfortable saying that accuracy matters more than speed.

So yes, in a sense, I do avoid rushing into stories when the facts are still thin, the technical details are incomplete, or the public conversation is running on assumptions.

That is not fear. That is discipline.

One of the worst habits in tech and security media right now is the pressure to have a complete opinion before complete information exists. That is how bad takes spread. That is how weak analysis gets published. That is how people start confusing confidence with competence.

There is nothing wrong with taking a beat.

### “If you saw the story, why not at least do a quick post?”

Because a quick post is still a post.

It still takes attention. It still takes space. It still adds another voice to the pile. And if all I am doing is repeating the same surface-level summary that ten other people already posted, that is not a great use of anybody’s time, including mine.

I am not interested in publishing filler.

I would rather write one piece that says something real than five pieces that just prove I was online.

### “Your posts feel more strategic than reactive. Is that intentional?”

Very.

One of the biggest reasons I do not write about every single story is because I am usually looking for the pattern behind the headline.

A vendor issue is rarely just a vendor issue. A breach is rarely just one company’s problem. A bad default, a weak design decision, a preventable exposure, a rushed AI rollout, a sloppy cloud posture, a trust failure, an identity gap, a browser change, a supply chain problem, a training issue, a management failure, a policy problem, a communication breakdown. These things tend to connect.

That is the part I care about most.

I want to ask:

- What does this mean for defenders?
- What does this mean for small teams?
- What does this mean for MSPs?
- What does this mean for the people actually responsible for keeping systems running and users protected?
- What does this tell us about the direction things are heading?

That kind of writing is naturally less reactive and more analytical.

That is on purpose.

### “Are you moving away from news entirely?”

No. Not at all.

News still matters. I still write about it. I still pay attention to it every day. It absolutely shapes what I think, what I research, and what I choose to publish.

But I want to engage with news on my terms.

That means I am more likely to write when:

- I can add context
- I can connect it to real-world operations
- I can explain the broader security implications
- I can bring in practitioner perspective
- I can turn it into something educational or strategically useful

If a story gives me a strong angle, I will absolutely take it.

If it does not, I am comfortable letting it pass.

### “Does working in the industry change how you choose what to write?”

Absolutely.

When you work in this space, you start seeing how different the real world is from the online conversation.

Not every headline turns into a meaningful operational concern.
Not every “massive” story changes anything for most organizations.
Not every viral post reflects what defenders are actually dealing with.
And not every security problem deserves the same amount of attention.

Sometimes the most important things are not the loudest things.

Working in industry gives you a different filter. You start asking harder questions. You start looking for impact, not just novelty. You start caring less about whether something is trending and more about whether it changes risk, operations, policy, trust, or implementation.

That is a much better foundation for writing.

### “So what kind of posts should people expect from you?”

The honest answer is this:

You should expect posts that try to be worth your time.

Sometimes that will look like a strategic analysis of a major security story once the bigger picture becomes clear. Sometimes it will look like a professional take shaped by hands-on industry experience. Sometimes it will be a tutorial, a how-to, a workflow, a defensive recommendation, or a broader editorial about where I think the industry is going.

What you probably should not expect is for me to write something every single time cybersecurity Twitter, Mastodon, LinkedIn, or the news cycle decides that everybody needs to react *right now*.

That is just not how I want to work.

## The bigger point

I think there is real value in slowing down enough to have an actual opinion.

Not a performative opinion.
Not an algorithm-driven opinion.
Not a “say something before people forget this topic exists” opinion.

An actual opinion.

One built on experience, pattern recognition, professional judgment, and enough distance from the noise to tell what matters and what does not.

That is what I try to do here.

I do not always get it perfectly right. No one does. But I would rather be thoughtful than frantic. I would rather be selective than shallow. I would rather publish with purpose than publish from pressure.

So if you have ever wondered why I did not write about a certain breach, vulnerability, vendor mess, browser issue, AI security story, or whatever else was dominating the feed that day, that is why.

I am not trying to cover everything.

I am trying to cover the things where I can bring something useful to the table.

And honestly, I think the internet could use a little more of that.
